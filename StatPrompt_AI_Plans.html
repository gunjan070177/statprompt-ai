How to read this story like a statistician

In plain words

Economists expect prices to be rising faster in September than in August: about 5.4% above a year earlier, versus 4.82%. Until the official figure is released, 5.40% is a prediction.

How big is it, really?

+0.58 pp is a 12% relative rise in the rate, and the forecast is still 0.60 pp under the 6% upper band used in this story. Percentage points measure distance; percent measures proportional change. They answer different questions.

What it does NOT say

It does not say prices rose 5.4% since August, because the rate compares with the same month last year. It does not say every household faces 5.4%, and 41 forecasters are a panel, not a random sample.

Common misreading

“Inflation jumped 0.58%.” The unit is wrong: it is 0.58 percentage points. A second slip is treating the consensus as the official result.

Check yourself

If the official print is 5.9%, by how many points did the consensus miss, and is that large?

Show the answer

The miss is 5.9 − 5.40 = +0.50 pp. Whether that is large depends on the usual forecast error, which this story does not give. In the Scenario Lab’s default (assumed SD 0.30 pp) it is about 1.7 SDs.

2 · How the statistic is produced

Purchasing Managers’ Index readings come from surveys of firms’ purchasing managers about changes from the prior month. A diffusion index summarizes shares reporting improvement, no change and deterioration, typically index = % higher + 0.5 × % unchanged. The published composite index aggregates sector survey results using the provider’s methodology; response shares and sector weights are not available in this article.

How to read this story like a statistician

In plain words

A PMI summarises one survey question: is activity higher, the same or lower than last month? A reading above 50 means more firms report improvement than deterioration.

How big is it, really?

With index = % higher + 0.5 × % unchanged, the index equals 50 + 0.5 × (% higher − % lower). So 53.1 means roughly 6.2 more points of firms report growth than contraction (2 × 3.1).

What it does NOT say

It does not say output grew 53.1%, and it does not measure the size of the change, only the direction firms report. Composite weights and response counts are not in the article.

Common misreading

Reading index points as percent. Another is over-reading the 0.1-point gap between composite (53.1) and services (53.0), which is within rounding.

Check yourself

If 40% of firms report higher, 40% unchanged and 20% lower, what is the index?

Show the answer

40 + 0.5 × 40 = 60. As a check, 50 + 0.5 × (40 − 20) = 60.

How to read this story like a statistician

In plain words

Electricity demand in Southeast Asia is projected to grow about 5.4% a year on average through 2030. That is a rate of growth, not a volume of electricity.

How big is it, really?

Over four years the multiplier is 1.054⁴ ≈ 1.234, or +23.4%. That is more than 4 × 5.4 = 21.6% because each year builds on a bigger base. At 5.4%, demand doubles in roughly 13 years.

What it does NOT say

It does not say demand grows exactly 5.4% every year, and it gives no TWh figure because no baseline was supplied.

Common misreading

Adding growth rates across years instead of multiplying factors, and reading a projection as a measurement.

Check yourself

Starting from an index of 100, what is demand after three years at 5.4% a year?

Show the answer

1.054³ ≈ 1.1709, so about 117.1. The 100-unit start is an illustration, not an observed dataset.

2 · How the statistic is produced

Each institution builds country-by-country projections and combines them into a world growth rate. The two aggregate with different country weights (commonly purchasing-power-parity weights at the IMF and market-exchange-rate weights at the World Bank), use different data cut-off dates and make their own assumptions about conflict, energy prices and trade. 2 · How the statistic is produced

For a share p from a simple random sample, the 95% margin of error is 1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n). Pollsters then adjust for weighting and design effects, which can make the published figure larger. The margin applies to each candidate’s share. A lead is the difference between two shares in the same sample, so its uncertainty is roughly twice as large and must be checked separately.

3 · Reproduce the arithmetic
Ohio simple-random-sample margin (p = 0.5)1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 616) ≈ 3.95 pp, versus 4.5 published → design effect ≈ (4.5 ÷ 3.95)² ≈ 1.30
New York simple-random-sample margin1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 900) ≈ 3.27 pp, matching the 3.3 published
Ohio: 95% margin on the lead1.96 × √[(0.49 + 0.45 − 0.04²) ÷ 616] ≈ 7.7 pp (≈ 8.7 pp after the design effect)
New York: 95% margin on the lead1.96 × √[(0.52 + 0.40 − 0.12²) ÷ 900] ≈ 6.2 pp
4 · Statistical tools to apply

Confidence intervals for proportions, standard error of a difference, design effects and weighting, subgroup margins of error, poll averaging.

5 · Decision-making insights

Ohio’s 4-point lead is smaller than its margin on a lead (about 8–9 points), so the poll cannot rule out a tie or a reversal. New York’s 12-point lead is about twice its roughly 6-point threshold. “Within the margin of error” means the data cannot separate the candidates, not that the race is a coin flip. A poll is also a snapshot, not a forecast.

Percentage points vs percent

A difference between two rates is in points; a relative change divides by the base. Mixing them is the most common slip in headlines.

A forecast is not an outcome

Forecasts carry error, and a stated error band is the honest way to read them. Story 09 shows one that held; story 06 shows two that disagree.

Surveys are samples

Employer surveys, household surveys, polls and PMIs all have sampling error, weighting choices and revisions.

Jobs lost and unemployment rate; TWh and growth rate. Read both, and ask what each divides by.

50 for a PMI, a consensus for jobs, a 50-year average for rainfall, an earlier forecast for revisions. “High” and “low” only mean something against a baseline.

Questions

Is this a real checkout?

No. This is a design prototype. Choosing a plan switches it on in this browser so you can preview each experience, and no payment is taken.

Who qualifies for Pro Student?

Anyone currently enrolled at a school, college or university. The plan is designed so verification happens before the first charge.

What happens to my progress if I switch plans?

Nothing is deleted. Scores and quiz progress stay in this browser. Moving down a plan only hides the Pro chapters, stories and Interpretation Desks.

Can I move from Pro Student to Pro Annual?

Yes, at any time. In this prototype the change is instant.

Prototype note. Prices (₹199 a month, ₹1,999 a year) and the Annual extras (capstone, certificate, priority replies) are proposals to edit before launch.
